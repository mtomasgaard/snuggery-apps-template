About this data

Not a navigation tool. The map and the heights are Kartverket's, read live from its services; the cabins and trails change, and a marked route on a screen is not a safe route on the day. Carry a map and a compass, and check the conditions before you go.

What the picture is

Kartverket's topographic map, draped over Kartverket's terrain model. In 3D the ground has its true shape: within a few kilometers of the view, the 1 m laser model (DTM1), read at up to 1.14 m a pixel; farther out, the 10 m model. Heights are not exaggerated, and the ground bends away with the earth.

With Sun on, every slope is lit by the real sun for the date and the time shown, computed for the middle of the view, and the shadows the mountains cast are swept over the heights the app holds around it: at 6 to 650 m a cell, finer the closer you look, with the earth's curvature. It is terrain shadow only: no cloud, haze, trees or buildings, and the sun is treated as a point.

The track under the picture is the signature of this app, the skyline. For the marker (the middle of the view, or a place you tapped) it draws the horizon the terrain makes in the direction of the sun at each minute of the day, 0.55 px for each degree above level, and over it the sun's path at the same scale. Where the sun's line clears the skyline, the marker is in direct sun. The horizon is read from the heights the app holds out to 60 km, with the earth's curvature and ordinary refraction; it is not a forecast of sunshine.

The surface is the map, a relief (the ground shaded from the heights alone, the water read from the map's own blue) or the ground colored by its height with one of three tables, Crameri's batlow and oleron or viridis, over the heights in view or over 0 to 2 500 m; the key under the picture prints the range. Kartverket publishes no topographic map without its names, so the relief and the elevation surfaces are the ones without draped names; the floating names come from OpenStreetMap.

Steepness colors the ground in the classes Norway's avalanche-terrain maps use, 27 to 30°, 30 to 35°, 35 to 40°, 40 to 45° and steeper, from the slope of the terrain model at up to 1 m near the view (coarser farther out, where it reads gentler than it is). It is not avalanche advice: it does not know the snow, the wind or the runout below a slope.

Snow is seNorge's modeled snow depth for the day shown (NVE and MET Norway, a 1 km grid, from the weather and a snow model, not from a satellite), drawn whiter the deeper it is and shed by walls; or a snow line you set, which this app draws lower on north faces and higher on south faces by up to 140 m, and never on faces steeper than about 55°.

The plate keeps one daylight look in both of the phone's themes; only the chrome around it follows the theme. That is a display choice, not data.

How the data gets here

This app is online: Snuggery lets it show pictures from the five sites it lists, once you allow them, and nothing else. The map, the trails, the heights and the snow arrive as pictures, at most six at a time, and the heights are read back out of their pixels; the cabins travel inside the app. Nothing you do is sent anywhere except the address of each picture, which says which piece of Norway is being looked at.

Solar position uses the NOAA Solar Calculator algorithm (Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms), written out in this app. Times are Norwegian, CET and CEST in summer, from the European rule, not from this phone's clock.

The 2D and 3D keys at the foot of the picture switch between the map and the terrain in one tap, keeping the place, its scale, the marker, a viewshed or a profile, and the layers. On the map, drag with one finger to move it, pinch to zoom toward your fingers, and double-tap to zoom in on a place. In 3D, drag with one finger to turn the view (sideways) and tilt it (up and down) around the ground in the middle of the picture; pinch to zoom toward your fingers; drag two fingers together to move across the land, which stays under them; double-tap to fly to a place, which becomes the new middle. A flick keeps the view moving for a moment. The camera stays above the ground, and the tilt stops before the horizon reaches the middle of the picture. Tap a cabin for its details, or the ground to put the marker there. The last key in the column, or the F key, hides the controls; the corner key that stays, or Escape, brings them back.

While moving

Off, the picture stays as sharp while you move it as when it stands still, and the land just beyond the view is loaded ahead. On, it is drawn at a lower resolution and with coarser terrain while it moves, the shadows wait until it stops, and nothing is loaded ahead: less power and data, a softer picture until the view rests.
